Pre-construction marketing

How I launch a community

Most projects start marketing when the building is done. On every community I launched, it started the day the concept existed, and the doors opened to a line.

For ten years at GL Homes I launched communities before they existed: master plans in Port St. Lucie, a town center that wasn't built yet, neighborhoods that were a rendering and a dirt road. Over a dozen of them, start to finish.

They all went through the same three phases. What we showed people and what we told them changed at each one, and every piece of it pointed at one day.

Three phases, then it's live

Phase 1

Pre-launch

The community exists on paper. A community page says what it is, where it is, what it will offer, and a price range at most. A lifestyle page shows the amenities in detail. Both carry a sign-up form.

Every sign-up joins a list that hears from us weekly from day one: aerial progress, the clubhouse breaking ground, the entrance going in. People watch it get built.

Phase 2

Launch

The plans are approved. Floor plans go up: renderings, beds and baths, one story or two. Price still reads "coming soon." Now the site has product.

The list gets the plan releases and plan highlights, and then the push. The grand opening goes on the community page, on the homepage, and out to every list we have.

Phase 3

Grand opening

The models are built. The day before, renderings swap for photographs and prices go on everything. Lots open.

The list shows up. Hundreds of people, vendors, food, billboards, television. When demand is there it runs as a lottery. Everything since the first sign-up was for this morning.

Live

The page stays alive, and so does the list

Move-in-ready homes, offers, progress and news. Each lot release, each price change, each amenity finished goes out as an email. The form stays up. Chat, email and phone route to the sales office, who qualify people and hand them to an agent on site.

Then the next community starts at phase one.

Phase 0 · when it's needed

Winning the neighbors first

Some projects need the city or the neighbors before any of this can start. For those we built a separate site for the people who had to say yes: what they'd get, what it would be, how it affected them.

We needed it in Boca Raton when GL bought a golf course. GL is doing it again right now in Kendall, on a former golf course in Miami-Dade.

The page grows and the list grows with it. Every step is triggered by something the project produces anyway: a concept, a set of plans, a finished model, an open door. Nothing has to be invented for marketing's sake.

What it did

1,200 to 7,000people on the interest list before opening, hearing from us every week

Where the examples sit

One reader

Thirty-two entitled acres and a rendering. The whole site was built for one person, the executive deciding whether to bring a company there. Location, demographics, a form to the project manager.

The whole cycle, repeated

Riverland is one master plan with a new sub-community every year or two, each running phases one through three. The coming-soon page is where every one of them starts.

Yaeger Plaza

Yaeger is in phase one, with about fourteen months until the doors open. The story so far has been told to the county, the city and Liberty City, and told well. Where it goes from here, and who it needs to reach next, is what I'd like to work out with you.
